The middle ages were time of the siege warfare. It is an interesting bit of trivia that sieges were much more common than battles in the field.
It is fitting to make a siege the theme of this session.
It is also very related as a topic because of the well-known Two Generals problem. The problem is though to mean that consistency in a distributed system is impossible.
The idea is that two armies are encamped on the opposite sides of a castle. If any one attacks alone, they lose. They need to coordinate the attack
The challenge is that the only way of communication between generals are messangers and the defenders are trying to shoot them down
Each messanger has certain change of getting through but the general who sent them does not know if they succeeded
It is fairly easy to see that neither general can even be sure that they are both in agreement when to attack
In the distributed system terms it translates to the impossibility of having exactly-once delivery semantics: to have a message delivered only once to the processing code.
Let's leve the generals for a moment and look at how that relates to real-life distributed systems
It turns out that the two generals problem differs in one more aspect from the message processing in async systems. In the latter we usually have three things that needs coordination: consumption of the incoming message, persisting the state change and sending out messages.
Moreover, the three resources we are coordinating have sligthly different characteristics
While we are here let me also mention that when I use term transaction I mean the entirety of processing of a single message, no a transaction with a data store or a queue. For these I'll use the term operation

So how exactly can we create a communication plan using messangers to make sure attacks succeed?
We need to also keep in mind that, thanks to not exactly very healthy medieval diet, they all have very bad memory.
So bad in fact, that Blue and Red can't remember anything 10 seconds after reading a messanger's note.
Let's put the green general in charge. He needs to know that the others received his orders before he marches with his troops to battle
The red one should never ever attack first. He needs confirmation from the blue one.
The blue one needs to keep track of orders received from green. He checks if we send his troops for a given charge. If so, he should not send them again. Once he sent his troops, he should immediately inform the red general by sending messangers to him until he gets the confirmation.
We need some sort of a protocol between Green and Blue to uniquely identify attack waves.

















As we can see, most of the burden is on the blue general. It has to maintain information about messages already received from the Green one and make sure to forward them to the Red one.
Our message processing logic is the Blue General
But while the blue general had to only keep track of attack waves, our processing logic needs to also do something useful -- maintain and update business data.
This is why we have two separate arrows here (although everything needs to happen atomically)
Not only that, the messages we send out can depend on the state of the stored business data
So we need to store the messages we are about to send, alongise the identification of the message we finished processing
The blue general's tent would be full of books, not weapons
Each entry containing information about the "message" it processed, including:
Unique ID - order 56
Date - October 12
Messages to be sent to the Red general
This is in essence the outbox pattern as it is used in the messaging systems.
The important bit is it always includes the information about the incoming messages so that messages can be de-duplicated

We need to think outside of our era!
The idea of outbox is based on tracking what we processed in order to avoid duplicate processing
We can achieve the same effect by tracking what is left to be processed
But in this second approach, we leave no garbage behind
Let's call these to-be-processed tracking devices tokens. You can only process a message if you have a token for it.
The important caveat is that we still need the transaction metadata information in the database. The difference is that we'll need it only for a very short time while the transaction is being worked on.
Let’s see what receiver would look like: it needs a one time token that it deletes when processing a message
When it receives a message, it needs to confirm it can obtrain a token for processing it (top "white" database) before actually processing it
In reality, it needs to follow a more elaborte list of 5 precisely defined steps

First we ensure our records are created. It does not matter which of the concurrent threads does it.
Then the real processing starts by loading the existing record and ensuring it has no information about being already processed
The processing ends by updating the record with indication it has already been processed IF AND ONLY IF it does not have that indication yet -- optimistic concurrency
